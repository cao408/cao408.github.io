title: L4+ 自动驾驶的条件、环境属性与智能体架构
date: 2026-10-30

在现行分级制度下讨论 L4+，真正决定难度的不是它比 L4 高一级，而是它取消了设计运行范围，也就是系统被允许在哪些道路、天气与速度条件下工作。L4 的可靠性建立在范围之内，范围内它承担全部驾驶任务并能自行兜底，范围外则必须停止运行、安全停车；L4+ 把这个范围整体取消，任何道路、任何天气、任何地点都要能够胜任，车上甚至不必有驾驶员。因此问题的性质从在给定领域内把错误率压到足够低，变为在无界领域内论证风险存在上界。前者是典型的机器学习问题，后者是智能体设计与验证问题。

驾驶环境对智能体的约束，可以先归为四类。第一类是不可见性。对自动驾驶车辆而言，环境是部分可观测的，遮挡之后有什么、邻车是否意图并线，传感器并不完全给出，系统只能靠推断来决定行为；同一处遮挡，人靠经验补全，机器只能靠对不确定性的显式建模。第二类是随机性。其他交通参与者既随机又带理性，行为的组合空间无法穷举，预测只能给出分布而非确定值。第三类是延时性。安全边界以毫秒计，时速一百公里时一次百毫秒级的迟疑就换来近三米的位移，而从察觉险情到车辆停稳通常需要七十米左右的行程，几乎没有留出深思的余地，这直接排除了大模型慢慢想清楚再动作的做法。第四类是代价高昂。碰撞的后果不可逆，系统无法像下棋那样靠输棋学会下棋，而真实数据里致命场景本就稀少，靠采集难以补上。

这四类约束合起来要求智能体同时做到反应快、有预判、能博弈，还得在从未出现过的场景里不失控。已有架构的谱系恰好在这几项上各有偏废。反应式架构不建世界模型，感知直接接通动作，实时性最好，却因为没有模型而无从预判，在博弈与长尾面前近乎盲目。慎思式架构以显式符号建模见长，可解释、能推理，但规则终究写不完，开放世界里总有未写到的情形，实时性也不足。混合三层架构靠纵向分层兼顾快与慢，是工业界主流，代价是层间优先级与仲裁机制要人手工设计，两层指令相左时缺少裁决依据。学习型与端到端方法用数据换覆盖，上限高，却是黑盒，无法验证，分布外没有保证，而取消范围之后几乎事事都落在分布之外。大模型智能体在常识与语义理解上最强，能读懂施工告示、交警手势这类符号化的意外，但存在幻觉，不能保证绝对的安全，而且时延偏高，可靠性达不到毫秒级的要求。

这些偏废指向同一个结论，把希望寄托在更强的单一模型上在目前看来不现实。L4+ 对可信度的高要求使得架构只能采取分层与责任分离的形态。感知交给学习模块，以应对开放世界；预测采用概率模型，以刻画他人意图的随机性；战术决策使用规则，以保证过程可审计；最底层保留一个确定性安全模块，并用数学方法证明它在给定输入下必然把车辆带入最小风险状态。这一层不宜交给任何学习模块，因为它的职责是证明，不是表现。分层在这里不是工程上的折中，而是把能够学习的东西与必须能够证明的东西分开摆放。

反思机制则决定了系统能否发现自身的缺陷，并在此基础上继续优化。在已有的实践上，它可以沿三个时间尺度展开。行驶中进行在线自我监控，置信度不足便减速靠边、主动降级，这是对不可见这一约束的保守应对。作出决定之前，用世界模型把候选动作未来的后果先行推演，再择优执行，这是把慎思压缩进实时约束的办法。事后则依靠离线闭环：以影子模式在常规运行中采集人工接管与险情片段，提取为场景，在仿真环境中复现，归因到感知、预测或决策中的某一环，再补数据或改规则，最后用回归测试确认修好。离线这一层之所以必需，恰恰因为第四类约束不允许在线试错，学习只能发生在离线，反思是它唯一的通道。

我认为此处讨论的价值不在于让策略变得更聪明，而在于失败可以被归因、进步可以被度量。开放世界的场景不可能被完全覆盖，这对模型的泛化能力提出了要求，而系统能给出的只有风险的上界。L4+ 需要一套能持续发现新场景的机制，让覆盖率不断逼近但不能宣告完成。它还需要考虑最坏情况，由一层可形式化验证的模块保证车辆必然进入最小风险状态，让极端情况下的损失可控。除此之外，剩余风险也必须被量化、申报并接受监管核对，否则决策由哪一层做出就成了没有答案的问题。

*English*

What makes L4+, the step above L4, difficult is not that it stands one level higher. It is that the operating envelope is cancelled: the set of roads, weather and speed conditions under which the system is permitted to work disappears. The reliability of L4 rests inside that envelope. Within it the system performs the whole driving task and provides its own fallback; outside it, the vehicle must stop operating and come to a safe halt. L4+ removes the envelope altogether, so that every road, every kind of weather and every location must be handled, and there need not be a driver on board at all. The problem therefore changes in kind, from holding the error rate low enough inside a given domain to establishing an upper bound on risk across an unbounded one. The former is a machine learning problem in the ordinary sense; the latter is a problem of agent design and verification.

The constraints the driving environment places on an agent can be grouped into four kinds. The first is invisibility. For an automated vehicle the environment is only partially observable: what lies behind an occlusion, whether the neighbouring car intends to merge, is not fully delivered by the sensors, and the system must decide on the basis of inference. Faced with the same occlusion, a person fills the gap from experience; a machine can do so only through explicit modelling of uncertainty. The second is randomness. Other road users are at once stochastic and rational, the space of their joint behaviour cannot be enumerated, and prediction can yield a distribution rather than a definite value. The third is delay. Safety margins are measured in milliseconds: at a hundred kilometres per hour, a hesitation of a hundred milliseconds already costs nearly three metres of travel, while bringing the vehicle to a stop from the moment a hazard is noticed typically takes some seventy metres, leaving almost no room for deliberation. This rules out the practice of letting a large model think the matter through before it acts. The fourth is cost. A collision is irreversible, and a system cannot learn to drive as it learns to play chess, by losing games; fatal scenarios are rare in real data to begin with, and collection alone will not make up the deficit.

Together these four constraints require the agent to react quickly, to anticipate, to engage in strategic interaction, and still not lose control in situations it has never met. The existing families of architecture each fall short on one or another of these points. Reactive architectures build no world model and wire perception directly to action, which gives the best real-time behaviour; but no model means no anticipation, and they are nearly blind before interaction and long tails. Deliberative architectures, with their explicit symbolic models, are interpretable and capable of inference, yet their rules are ultimately incomplete, for the open world always contains cases not yet written down, and real-time performance suffers. Three-layer hybrid architectures reconcile speed and deliberation by stacking layers vertically and are the mainstream of industry, at the cost of hand-designed priorities and arbitration, so that when two layers issue conflicting commands there is no basis for adjudication. Learned and end-to-end methods trade data for coverage and reach a high ceiling, but they are black boxes that cannot be verified and offer no guarantee outside the training distribution, and once the envelope is removed almost everything lies outside it. Large-model agents are strongest in common sense and semantic understanding, able to read a roadworks sign or a traffic officer's gesture, yet they hallucinate, cannot guarantee absolute safety, and their latency is too high for reliability at the millisecond scale.

These shortcomings point to one conclusion: at present it is unrealistic to rest one's hopes on a stronger single model. The high demand L4+ makes on trustworthiness leaves architecture no choice but a layered form with separated responsibilities. Perception goes to learned modules, to cope with the open world; prediction uses probabilistic models, to capture the randomness of other agents' intentions; tactical decisions use rules, so that the process can be audited; and the bottom layer keeps a deterministic safety module whose behaviour is proved mathematically to bring the vehicle into a minimal risk state for any given input. That layer should not be handed to any learned module, because its job is proof, not performance. The layering here is not an engineering compromise but a separation between what can be learned and what must be provable.

A reflection mechanism then determines whether the system can discover its own defects and improve on them. In existing practice it unfolds over three time scales. During driving, online self-monitoring reduces speed and pulls over, degrading deliberately when confidence is insufficient, which is a conservative answer to invisibility. Before a decision is taken, a world model rolls out the consequences of candidate actions and the best is executed, which is how deliberation is compressed into a real-time constraint. Afterwards comes the offline loop: shadow mode captures human takeovers and near misses during routine operation, these are extracted as scenarios and replayed in simulation, the failure is attributed to perception, prediction or decision, data or rules are amended accordingly, and regression testing confirms the repair. This offline layer is necessary precisely because the fourth constraint forbids trial and error online: learning can happen only offline, and reflection is its only channel.

I take the value of the discussion here to lie not in making the policy cleverer but in making failure attributable and progress measurable. Scenarios in the open world can never be covered completely, which places demands on generalization, and the most a system can offer is an upper bound on risk. L4+ needs a mechanism that keeps discovering new scenarios, so that coverage approaches completeness without ever being declared complete. It must also take the worst case into account, with a formally verifiable module guaranteeing that the vehicle reaches a minimal risk state and keeping the loss in extreme situations bounded. Beyond that, residual risk has to be quantified, declared and opened to regulatory scrutiny; otherwise the question of which layer made a given decision has no answer.
